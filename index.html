Triage of public-facing telemetry ingestion endpoints accepting unauthenticated writes — and the data-provenance question that determines what it means.

Executive Summary

Multiple production telemetry collectors across major ecosystems accept synthetic, externally generated events without authenticating the producer as an authoritative source. That fact alone does not establish downstream compromise — but it opens the critical question this research is built around:

If unverified telemetry is retained, do server-controlled provenance controls remain durable and machine-actionable through every downstream stage — or can attacker-supplied records silently acquire the trust characteristics of verified observations?

The decisive property is a server-authoritative distinction between verified and unverified producers that survives normalization, storage, transformation, aggregation, export, alerting, and model pipelines. When that distinction is lost, telemetry injection becomes a data-provenance and trust-boundary failure — not merely "fake analytics."

Scope note: this site's documents distinguish confirmed ingestion-boundary behavior from downstream effects that cannot be independently verified from an external position. They do not claim that every unauthenticated telemetry collector is vulnerable, nor that every tested platform stores untrusted records with trusted records.

Method & Boundaries

  • Accepted ≠ retained. An HTTP 200 / itemsAccepted response demonstrates ingestion semantics, not permanent storage or trusted downstream use.
  • Parsed text ≠ execution. Caller-controlled fields accepted into a schema are data unless execution is separately demonstrated.
  • AI poisoning requires downstream evidence — the risk is architectural, not a confirmed finding per target.
  • Unique harmless canaries were preferred over destructive payloads; canary tracing is the recommended bridge to vendor-side validation.
  • No exploit code, no instructions enabling harm. Public-interest disclosure only.

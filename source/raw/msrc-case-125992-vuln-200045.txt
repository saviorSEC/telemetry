Description

The OneCollector telemetry pipeline accepts unauthenticated POST requests with the client-id=NO_AUTH parameter. Two endpoints (browser.events.data.microsoft.com and vortex.data.microsoft.com) return 204 No Content for arbitrary telemetry events with no authentication required.

A global API key (69adc3c768bd4dc08c19416121249fcc-66f1668a-797b-4249-95e3-6c6651768c28-7293) was discovered in the client-side source code of login.microsoftonline.com. This key can be used to inject arbitrary events into Microsoft's telemetry pipeline.

The injection supports:

    Basic event injection
    Bulk injection (10/10 events accepted)
    Large payload injection (50KB+)
    Steganographic C2 channels (commands hidden in ai.application.ver)
    Custom event properties

This is the same vulnerability pattern as VULN-193698 (App Insights injection), but affects a different telemetry pipeline.

Hello MSRC Team,

Thank you for providing Microsoft’s final assessment.

I understand Microsoft’s stated position to be that:

    OneCollector intentionally accepts telemetry from broad client environments;
    Backend validation, filtering, and trust-handling controls exist;
    Microsoft evaluated whether attacker-controlled events could bypass those controls and be consumed as trusted downstream data;
    Microsoft did not observe that outcome based on the evidence reviewed; and
    Microsoft considers the reported condition Low severity and does not plan further servicing.

I will represent that position accurately in my publication.

However, several matters require clarification for the upcoming disclosure record.

Classification inconsistency

Microsoft previously stated that this case did not meet its definition of a security vulnerability and was below the servicing threshold.

The latest response states that the issue is Low severity.

Please clarify which of the following is Microsoft’s final classification:

    A security vulnerability classified as Low severity;
    A non-security issue with Low operational impact;
    Intended behavior that does not constitute a vulnerability; or
    Another classification.

Those positions are not interchangeable, and the publication must describe Microsoft’s final determination accurately.

Scope of Microsoft’s technical validation

Your response states that Microsoft “did not observe” a bypass of downstream trust controls.

Please clarify whether that conclusion was based on:

    An actual trace of the submitted canary events;
    Review of production telemetry records;
    Review of the OneCollector architecture and configuration;
    Controlled internal reproduction by the service owner;
    Confirmation that the events were discarded or quarantined;
    Confirmation that the events retained an immutable untrusted-provenance classification; or
    A general assessment of the system’s intended design.

Please also answer:

    Were the submitted canaries located?
    Were they discarded, quarantined, retained, aggregated, or forwarded?
    Do externally submitted events receive a server-controlled provenance classification?
    Is that classification unavailable for external callers to create, alter, or remove?
    Does it remain attached through storage, transformation, aggregation, and export?
    Are downstream consumers technically prevented from treating such events as authenticated or trusted telemetry?
    At what stage does filtering occur—before storage and aggregation, or afterward?

I am not requesting source code, proprietary detection rules, internal credentials, database names, or sensitive architecture. A high-level service-owner attestation would be sufficient.

Why the distinction matters

Backend cleanup is not equivalent to durable provenance enforcement.

If an unauthenticated event is stored with valid telemetry, contributes to an aggregate, or becomes available to a consumer before filtering, the integrity impact may occur before later cleanup.

The relevant question is therefore not merely whether Microsoft has filtering controls. It is whether unverified events remain technically distinguishable from valid events throughout the complete processing lifecycle.

Privacy and legal review

I am not asserting that the observed HTTP response independently proves a legal violation.

However, if unauthenticated telemetry is retained with valid telemetry, linked to identifiable users, accounts, devices, or online identifiers, or used to influence records concerning individuals, Microsoft’s privacy and legal teams should review the applicable accuracy, integrity, security, and rectification requirements.

Potentially relevant frameworks include:

    GDPR Articles 5(1)(d), 5(1)(f), 25, and 32, concerning accuracy, integrity, data protection by design, and security of processing;
    California Civil Code §§ 1798.100(e), 1798.106, and 1798.81.5, concerning reasonable security and correction of inaccurate personal information; and
    Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, where actual data-security practices or representations could constitute unfair or deceptive practices depending on the facts.

These provisions do not establish a violation merely because OneCollector accepted an event. Their relevance depends on Microsoft’s actual retention, attribution, segregation, and downstream use of the submitted data.

Please confirm whether Microsoft’s privacy, data-protection, or legal personnel reviewed this matter.

CERT/CC coordination and publication

This case is part of a broader cross-vendor investigation into unauthenticated telemetry ingestion, producer authenticity, and preservation of data provenance across systems operated by major technology vendors.

I am preparing a coordinated submission to CERT/CC and, where appropriate, CISA. The complete MSRC correspondence, including Microsoft’s classification and statements concerning backend controls, will be included in that coordination package.

This is not being presented as proof that Microsoft’s controls failed. It will be presented as:

    Confirmation that unauthenticated telemetry ingestion is intentional;
    Microsoft’s assertion that backend trust controls protect downstream use;
    Microsoft’s conclusion that no control-bypass outcome was observed;
    The absence or presence of any canary trace information Microsoft provides;
    The final severity and servicing determination; and
    The unresolved distinction between intended architecture and externally verifiable event disposition.

Publication of the consolidated research remains scheduled for:

September 5, 2026

Microsoft’s silence, closure of this case, or continued internal discussion will not constitute my agreement to extend that timeline.

No extension exists unless Microsoft submits a specific proposal and I expressly accept it.

The publication will redact credentials, unnecessary operational details, and personal information. It will distinguish confirmed external observations, Microsoft’s statements, technical inferences, and facts that could not be independently verified.

If Microsoft would like the relevant Microsoft section reviewed solely for factual accuracy before publication, please identify an authorized technical contact. Such review would not grant Microsoft editorial approval or automatically modify the publication date.

Please provide the requested classification and technical clarifications for the disclosure record.
